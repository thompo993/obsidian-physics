# excerpt from overleaf report 
Scintillation is the production of light as a result of excitation from ionising radiation \cite{beaulieuScintillationDosimetry2016}, in this case positrons emitted as a result of muon decay. To collect information about the positrons, a series of novel scintillating tiles have been designed and assembled for Super MuSR. They come in 30mm, 43mm, 63mm, 105mm and 210mm sizes in order to conserve solid angle irrespective of distance from the sample. The tiles are made of a base of Eljen 204 scintillator which has a long optical attenuation length and fast timing, ideal for the high rates of data collection \cite{EJ200EJ204EJ208}. 


The tile assembly process begins with inserting Wavelength shifting fibre (WLSF) into the scintillators machined central cavity. WLSF is bent at 90 degrees in two places. This allows light produced by the scintillator to be directed towards the active area of the SiPMs. Cracks and damage to the bends can cause poor light output, so bends are done using a specialised jig, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:fibre_bending_jig}, which is submerged in hot water and used to form the bends with minimal damage. 

The fibre and scintillator are held in place by a gluing/curing jig seen in Figure \ref{fig:gluing_curing_jig}, allowing the tiles to be in the correct position before being fixed in place by transparent epoxy. Bubbles and imperfections in the coupling will reduce light output, so the solution is to degas the epoxy in a vacuum chamber. This is a three step process where first the container of epoxy is degassed, and then the epoxy is injected into the cavity and filled to the top, placed into a vacuum chamber and twice more. Plastic studs are slotted on top of the WLSF for the purpose of mechanical strength to prevent the fibre from being bent during handling or installation, and also to provide a method to align the fibre and the SiPM active area. Then the epoxy is left to cure for 48 hours in a low temperature low humidity oven to set the fibre and studs to the whole scintillating tile.

The stud and WLSF will be of different lengths, and are set to deliberately be longer than necessary to allow for them to be lapped down using high grit micro-fine silicone carbide sandpaper, such that they have a polished finish. Each tile is placed into a lapping jig shown in Figure \ref{fig:lapping_jig}. The tile is fixed in place and polished down to the level of the bottom stainless steel plate.

The final stage of assembly is the wrapping state, this involves placing an Excellent Specular Reflector (ESR) is wrapped around the tile in order to minimise light loss, it is kept tightly wrapped by thin layers of aluminium tape, providing maximum reflection from the ESR.
