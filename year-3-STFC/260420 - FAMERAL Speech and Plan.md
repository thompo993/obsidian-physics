## Full Script
Nuclear fusion, MRI machines, quantum computing, and MagLev trains all rely on superconductors. But the real breakthrough would be a _room-temperature_ superconductor—something that could completely transform how we generate, transmit, and use energy.

So, what _is_ a superconductor?

A superconductor is a material that can carry electrical current with **zero resistance** below a certain critical temperature. The challenge is that this temperature is usually extremely low—often hundreds of degrees below freezing.

This remarkable phenomenon is governed by quantum mechanics, and for over a century, scientists have been searching for materials that could superconduct at room temperature. Recent progress suggests we may finally be getting closer.

But that raises a key question:

**How do we actually study what’s happening inside a superconductor?**

At ISIS, one of the main techniques we use is called **MuSR**—Muon Spin Rotation, Relaxation, and Resonance.

So how does MuSR work?

First, we need muons.

We produce them using a synchrotron, which accelerates protons to about **84% of the speed of light**. These protons collide with a carbon target, producing particles called pions, which quickly decay into muons.

A key feature of these muons is that they are **polarised**—we know their initial spin direction.

These muons are then guided along a beamline and implanted into our sample while still fully polarised.

Once inside the superconductor, the muons come to rest, but their spins don’t just sit still.

They **precess**.

You can think of them like tiny ballet dancers pirouetting within the material.

Because muons behave like tiny magnets, their precession is influenced by the local magnetic fields inside the superconductor.

By measuring how fast they precess, we can probe magnetism at the atomic scale.

But how do we actually _measure_ this?

Muons are unstable—they live for about **2.2 microseconds** before decaying. When they decay, they emit a positron in a direction related to their spin at that moment.

By detecting and counting these positrons, we can reconstruct how the muon spins evolved over time—and from that, determine the internal magnetic properties of the material.

So what does this tell us about superconductors?
Most practical superconductors allow strong magnetic fields it to penetrate in tiny, quantised tubes called **vortices**.

These vortices arrange themselves into an ordered lattice, creating a unique magnetic field pattern inside the material—something MuSR is perfectly suited to measure.

From this pattern, we can extract important properties such as the **superfluid density**, which tells us how strong and coherent the superconducting state is.

We can also see how ordered the vortex lattice is. If the pattern is distorted, it indicates defects in the material and something called **vortex pinning**.

Pinning is actually crucial for real-world applications, because it prevents vortices from moving—allowing superconductors to carry large currents without energy loss.

Techniques like MuSR are helping scientists peer inside superconductors in unprecedented detail, bringing us ever closer to unlocking their full potential—and perhaps in the near future, revolutionising energy with a room temperature superconductor
