# Notes from report:
The 210mm tiles exhibited a severe drop in light output for a group of four tiles (36, 37, 38 and 39), outlined in red in Figure 17a. In total there were 37 tiles that were investigated, with an average of 1.9% worse than the benchmark tile repeats. When removing the four extremely poor performing tiles, the mean becomes a 1.7% improvement above the benchmark mean. Neither one of these percentages raise concern on their own, as this is a very small deviation away from the benchmark tile, however as this decrease comes largely from outliers, they were investigated alongside the other 3 tiles that fell below the acceptance threshold:

Each of these tiles was noted to have an issue in a single stud, however so did the comparison tile that has performed above the acceptance threshold. No statistically significant difference between studs that were marked as damaged and those that were not was found (t-test, p=0.810, not significant). This means that stud damage cannot be solely responsible for the reduced light output of the investigated tiles. Figure A6 outlines the 2D stud analysis of all four of the tiles in Table 3. All three of the poorly performing tiles, and tile ID 34 exhibit asymmetry across one of the studs. Longer tiles experience greater levels of light attenuation and any small manufacturing defect has a longer length over which this defect can accumulate, resulting in asymmetry in each of the tile studs. Furthermore, the longer tiles may experience more trouble with optical coupling between the scintillator and WLSF due to the resin not flowing uniformly along the tile, resulting in non-uniform light output which would manifest in the form of asymmetrical light output, which was observed in the majority of 105mm tiles. SiPMs can be individually calibrated to account for a difference in light output between studs, so asymmetrical stud light output does not mean a tile cannot be used on the final detector. Another feature of note is "Chilled Resin" this refers to refrigerating the resin before using it, a process technicians integrated into the production process to improve the ease of handling of RT152 resin. Like the 30mm tiles, a negatively skewed Gaussian curve is obtained. The histogram is consistent with the pattern of most tiles being clustered near the maximum, and a long tail representing tiles with defects. For the 30mm tiles, this is due to the R&D process which is evidenced by the fact that there have not been any tiles past tile ID 100 that have failed, and tiles that fall below the acceptance threshold have done so in groups. For the case of the 105mm tiles the poorly performing tiles are spread uniformly throughout all tested tiles, suggesting that opposed to a systematic fault in production this is a feature of the randomness and difficulty of the production process. Overall the tiles were noted to have 7.3% failure rate, assuming all investigated tiles fail, with no clear indication of the cause of the drop-in performance. Current hypotheses attribute the reduction in light performance to a poor optical coupling between the WLSF and scintillator, caused by issues with the resin flowing non-uniformly across the length of the tile. It must also be noted that accounting for error these tiles may still have an acceptable degree of light output, and all 3 tiles fall within 5% of the benchmark mean. Therefore future work will involve re-testing these tiles in order to determine if they are suitable for the final detector.



No clear indication of faults in the four outlier tiles were found in the technicians notes, despite tile performance being 32.1% worse than the benchmark mean. They were also all from the same batch, so the decision to unwrap these tiles and inspect them was made. The inspection showed a very thin layer of resin along the top of the tile, illustrated in Figure A4. This is a mechanically suitable connection, as it holds the WLSF in place and is flush with the scintillator tile, allowing for the tile to be wrapped very easily and precisely. However, this appears to significantly reduce the light output, suggesting that conservative application of resin causes a much poorer optical coupling. Applying enough resin to the tiles such that it pools along the top of tiles guarantees the resin flows into the cavity between the WLSF and the scintillator along the whole length of the tile, forming a better optical collection and improving light output. In order to confirm this is the factor determining light output, the tiles were refilled and re-measured, as seen in Figure 17a. After refilling these tiles, they were re-measured, with an average improvement in light output of 17.4%. This strongly suggests that this is the reason for the failure of the 210mm tiles. However the refilled tiles did not recover all of the lost performance, and are still performing 20.3% worse than the benchmark mean. It is hypothesised that this is due to the interface between already cured and secondary applications of resin being less transparent than a single layer of resin. This could be due to grime, dust and other contaminants forming on the original application of resin. Furthermore this top up will not help the gaps between the WLSF and scintillator where the resin has already cured over the top, meaning some gaps cannot be improved by refilling the tiles. After this investigation all 210mm tiles were made with a larger amount of resin to ensure a strong optical coupling. Tile ID 078 is the first to have this larger amount of resin applied to it and exhibits a light output that has returned to above the benchmark tile, suggesting that the production team is now producing tiles that are now exceeding the acceptance threshold. Aside from the refilled tiles, tile IDs 9, 10 and 59 were noted to have damage to one or both of the studs, the 2D stud analysis (Figure A7) found that there was asymmetrical light output in tile IDs 10 and 78. No clear fault was found in Tile 78, and it is performing above the acceptance threshold and tile 10 had very loose stud. The histogram illustrates a negatively skewed distribution of tiles, with the leftmost tail being made of the four refilled tiles and tile ID 59. This represents the expected pattern of tiles being produced near the maximum possible light output with defects causing the left hand side tail of the distribution of the histogram. Unlike the 105mm tiles, the tiles that fall below the acceptance threshold do so more significantly compared to the 105mm tiles, of which all tiles were performing within 5% of the benchmark mean. Comparing this to the 210mm tiles, two tiles are within 10%, and one is within 20% of the benchmark mean (excluding the refilled tiles). This suggests that 210mm tiles are more greatly affected by defects compared to shorter tiles. Overall 18.9% of the 210mm tiles fell below the acceptance threshold, significantly higher than any other length of tile seen so far. However a more representative estimate of the failure rate going forward would be to exclude the four refilled tiles giving an overall failure rate of 8.8%.
